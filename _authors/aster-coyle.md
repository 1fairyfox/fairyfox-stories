Aster Coyle writes science fiction the way an engineer writes a fault report: plainly, in order, with
nothing decorative and nothing skipped. The sentences are short and load-bearing; the machinery of a
world is shown working rather than explained, so the reader learns a ship the way a crew member does —
by watching what it does and paying for what it costs. The tense is usually present, because Coyle's
stories are about pressure that is happening *now*, in a place with no outside to look back from.

The recurring subject is the sealed system — a ship, a station, a colony under a dome — and the cold
arithmetic that keeps it alive: rations, quittances, the exact number a closed world can bear. Coyle is
drawn to the person that arithmetic can't quite account for, and to the keeper of the count who has to
decide what a rule is finally *for*. The warmth is real but rationed: it stays under the procedural
surface for pages and then, at the chosen moment, comes up once, hard, and lands. Nothing is lurid;
the dread is the vertigo of scale and time and a system that erases what it cannot number.

*Writes in the Long Quiet.*
