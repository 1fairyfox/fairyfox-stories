For three days I kept the finest stall on the Salt Road, and I want to be honest about how good it felt,
because a tale that hides the pleasure of a wrong thing has not understood the wrong thing at all.

I counted my bottles twice a day still, out of the old habit, but the counting had changed its nature.
Before, it had been a fear, a man patting his coat for a purse that might have been cut. Now it was a
tally against a promise. Every bottle on my shelves was already sold, at three times its price, to a hand
that would come and pay before the gap could show. I was, for the first time in my life, a keeper who could
not be ruined by the sky. I had bought that with one signature and the loss of a trade I told myself I did
not need — the odd traveller with the odd copper, the walk-up want I would now have to wave off toward some
other, poorer stall down the Road.

I waved off three of them in those three days. A drover with a cracked axle who wanted a pushing-wind to
get him to the smith; a widow after a drying-wind for her one good rug; a boy who only wanted, I think, to
watch me uncork something. To each I said the bottles were spoken for, which was true, and each looked at
my full blue shelves and then at me, and went on. I told myself the arithmetic held. Triple price forever
against a handful of coppers walking away — the sum was not close, I had done it a hundred times, it came
out the same way every time. It is a strange thing about a sum that is always right. You can run it to
comfort yourself, and the more often it comes out in your favour the more you find you need to run it.

On the fourth morning a caravan came through from the west, bells going, alive with air the way a caravan
ought to be, and among the drovers was a lean brown boy I had known when we were both younger and stupider
and I had cost him a good deal of trouble on the way to costing myself more. Rafiq lifted a hand to me
across the yard and grinned and did not stop — a man with a train to keep to — and I lifted mine back, and
that was the whole of it, and it was enough. The living move on down the Road. It is the ones who stop at
your counter you have to reckon with.

She stopped at my counter a little after noon.

I saw the old man first, because she had set herself to hold him up and he was the larger of the two. He
was very old and had been strong once — you could read it in the ruin, the way you read a good boat in a
wreck. His skin had the weathering that only the coast gives, a brown gone grey and grained with salt, and
his eyes had the long focus of a man used to a horizon, and he was dying. I did not need telling. I have
kept a counter long enough to know the difference between a man who is tired and a man who is spending the
last of himself on purpose, and this one was spending, and had chosen where.

The girl was perhaps eleven. She was burned dark and worn thin by a great deal of walking, and she wore
the deliberate calm of a child who has decided that crying will not be permitted until the errand is done.
She got the old man settled on my step, in the shade of the snapping cloth, and then she came to the
counter and stood on her toes to see over it, and she put down her money.

She did not put it down the way people put down money. She laid it out. Coin by coin, in a row, in an
order she had clearly kept for a long time, so that the counting of it was already done before I could
begin — a small hard row of it, the exact fare and not one copper more, worn smooth-edged from handling.

"I have a hundred days of coins," she said. "I counted them in the dark so I would know them by their
edges. It is all here. I want the sea-wind, please. For my grandfather."

I said nothing for a moment, which she took, correctly, for listening, and went on.

"He was a fisherman. Before. He wants to smell the sea one more time, and we are a hundred days from any
sea, and he cannot walk it, so I came for the sea instead. They said on the Road there was a stall at the
ninth waystation that had a bottle of the coast in it. An old grey one, up high, that nobody ever bought
because nobody here wants the sea." She said it without any art at all. That was the terrible skill of her.
"Is it true? Do you have it?"

I did have it. It stood where it had stood for years, the third bottle on the high shelf, a grey salt
breath of a coast none of my travellers had ever seen, bought off a caravan-master who had died owing the
sea to no one but himself. I had thought of it as furniture. I had thought of it, three days before, with a
man's fair contract being read to me, as the one bottle I would never sell, and had let that thought stand
in for a reason to sign.

*I will never sell that one anyway. Who here wants the sea?*

Here, now, was who.

I want to set down exactly what was in me, because this is the part of the tale I have gone back to most,
and it is not the part with the crying in it. There was no crying yet. There was only the sum, and how
cleanly it came out.

If I sold her the sea-wind, I would lose the stall. Not a fine, not a scolding — the stall. The whole of
it. Its cloth, its shelves, its deed, reverted entire to Sabr, in full, for that one bottle. Hadi had
written it plainly and read it aloud so that I could not one day claim I had not understood, and I
understood. One bottle to any hand but his and the striped roof came down over my head and old Bostan's
forty years and the only table I had ever wanted were a stranger's to fold up and carry off. That was the
price of the sale.

And the price of the refusal was nothing.

That is what I need you to see. There was no trap in it, no cruelty I would have to perform, no lie I would
have to tell. The bottle was, by a contract I had signed with my own steady hand and my eyes open, not
mine to sell. I could say so, and it would be true. I could send the child back into the glare with her
hundred days of coins, and keep every bottle on my shelf, and keep my safe stall, and keep my good name,
which would remain good, because I would have done nothing at all but honour a fair agreement. No one on
the Road would fault me. Hadi Sarraf least of all — he had built the thing so that keeping it would feel
like virtue. I would lose nothing. I would be exactly as safe at sundown as I had been at noon.

I ran the sum. Everything to lose on the one side, nothing to lose on the other. It is the easiest sum
there is. A child could do it. I had done harder ones before breakfast.

"I'm sorry," I said. "That one is promised."

She did not argue. I had braced for argument, the clever kind, the wheedling — I am good with a haggler,
I have been one — but she had none in her. She only looked at me the way you look at a door you have walked
a hundred days to reach and found barred, which is to say not with anger, because anger is for things you
still believe you can change, but with a kind of grave re-reckoning, a child counting her coins over again
in case the world had misheard them.

"It is the right money," she said, uncertain now, thinking the fault must be in the fare. "I can count it
for you."

"The money is right," I said. "The money is more than right. It isn't the money."

"Then—" She stopped. She looked at the high shelf, at the grey bottle catching the noon exactly as it had
caught it while a man read me a clause about it, and she looked back at me, and I watched her decide not to
understand, because understanding would cost more than she had left. "All right," she said. "Thank you for
looking."

She had thanked me. That is a thing I have not managed to put down anywhere it stops weighing.

She went and got the old man up off my step. He had not asked me for anything; he had let the child carry
the errand, the way the dying let the living do the last things, and now he let her lift him, and he looked
at me once over her head — not a beggar's look, nothing in it that asked me to reconsider, only a
fisherman's look, level, taking my measure the way he must once have taken a sky's — and then he turned it
away, out toward the west and the hundred days of nothing between him and the water, and the two of them
walked back down off my patch of shade into the white glare of the Road, the girl on the seaward side, her
shoulder under his arm, going slowly, going away.

I did not call after them. I want that in the ledger too. There was a long moment in which I could have,
and the shelves were full behind me, and the sum was still right, and I did not.

I went back in under my snapping cloth, into my safe stall that could not be ruined by any sky. I counted
the bottles. They were all there, every one, all promised, worth three times their price to a man who
would never open a single one, and I stood among them and felt for the held breath I had been living in for
three days, that lovely full safety, and could not quite find it. It had a hairline in it now, fine as the
crack that runs a bottle you can still, for a while, keep pouring from.

I did not have a name for what was wrong. I was good with names — names have weight on the Salt Road, and I
had made my living knowing them — but I turned this one over all afternoon and could not read its edges. It
was not guilt, quite; I had broken no promise, cheated no one, done nothing but keep a fair word fairly. It
was not the money; the money was never the trouble. It was only that I had been shown, in one small hard
row of coins and one grey bottle, the difference between a stall that cannot fail and a stall worth keeping,
and I had learned it the way you learn most true things, which is a little too late to un-know.

The sea-wind sat up on the high shelf where it had always sat, promised now, catching the last of the
light. I found I could not look at it and I found I could not stop, which is, I have come to think, the
exact condition of a person about to do something expensive.
